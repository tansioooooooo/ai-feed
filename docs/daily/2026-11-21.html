Today we are releasing Inkling 2 Mini, a fast, powerful model that’s small enough to run on your laptop.
Inkling 2 Mini is a looped transformer, running the middle layers of the model three times for every token. This allows the model to apply more reasoning and reach the right answer with fewer tokens, especially on tasks such as agentic coding that depend on reasoning quality. Inkling 2 Mini leads every open-weights model with less than 100B total parameters on benchmarks such as DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%). It runs faster than similarly performing models as well.
Inkling 2 Mini is a Mixture-of-Experts model with 26.4B total parameters, 3.4B active, and 5.4B weight activations per token to account for looping. Looping allows the model to reason longer without writing its reasoning down, which matters for monitoring: our ability to oversee and understand what the model does by reading its chain of thought (CoT).
We measured the impact of looping on both performance and monitorability with Inkling 2 Mini and a research-specific variable-loop variant we are releasing alongside it. The variable-loop model’s no-CoT time horizon improved from 16 seconds at one pass to 37 seconds with three, and its performance on a benchmark of serial reasoning kept improving out to ten loops—twice as many as it was trained with. The model’s chain of thought grew shorter with more loops but stayed legible throughout. The model didn’t trend higher on tests of agentic sabotage as loops were added.
We’re excited to offer some of the first models with looped architecture available to researchers. The variable-loop model was used for our monitorability experiments as it allows for cleaner comparisons between different looping depths.
Most of Inkling 2 Mini’s weights were trained and are deployed natively in NVFP4, with the last four layers in MXFP8. The total memory footprint of Inkling 2 Mini is ~20GB, allowing it to run on local devices such as consumer laptops and desktop AI computers. Our researchers took Inkling 2 Mini on a flight, where it ran offline on a MacBook Pro and built a playable flight simulator in a few minutes.
Inkling 2 Mini is released with open weights and is available on Tinker today for inference and customization.
Capabilities
Inkling 2 Mini’s strengths mirror those of Inkling 2 Small: it does best on long-horizon agentic work and is the best among open-weights models of similar size on most tasks we measured.
Inkling 2 Mini scores ahead of every comparable model on agentic coding benchmarks such as DeepSWE v1.1 and SWE-bench Pro v2, and is behind only Qwen3.8-27B on others. On PMPP-Hard, a kernel-optimization benchmark, it approaches the score of the much larger Inkling 2 Small.
While looping improves the model’s reasoning, it doesn’t enable its weights to store more facts—knowledge is mostly a function of total parameter count. Inkling 2 Mini’s score on AA-Omniscience, a measure of factuality, is in line with other models of the same size. Studying the variable-loop model, we saw that performances on long-context benchmarks improve with more passes, such as GraphWalks BFS and MRCR v2; but knowledge-intensive benchmarks doesn’t improve with more passes, such as AA-Omniscience, SimpleQA Verified, SimpleVQA, and WorldVQA.
Inkling 2 Mini overcomes the knowledge gap by efficiently using search and tools. It leads open-weights models of similar size on BrowseComp, DeepSearchQA, and MCP Atlas. It is also strong on knowledge work benchmarks that incorporate search and tool use.

Why there are still no non-WebKit browsers on iOS
Firefox on iPhone is growing fast across the world. This is especially true in the EU since 2024, when the EU Digital Markets Act (DMA) started requiring Apple to let people choose their default browser; millions have picked Firefox, and daily use on iOS has doubled. People want to choose the browser on their phone.
But there remains an important limitation to what is available on iPhones: Apple forces all browsers on iOS to be built on Apple’s own browser engine.
This has meant that companies like Mozilla cannot easily bring the full range of Firefox features and functionality to iPhone users because many of these are built within our browser engine, Gecko. iPhones and iPads are unique in having this restriction. On all other major operating systems, including Apple’s macOS, people can access the Gecko-powered version of Firefox.
As a result of the DMA, Apple began allowing alternative browser engines on iOS in the EU. It published a set of conditions that companies like Mozilla must comply with to bring their own browser engine. At the time we said Apple’s rules made it “as painful as possible” for alternatives like Gecko. In the meantime, Japan also introduced new rules requiring alternative browser engines on iOS. Apple applied very similar conditions in Japan to those in the EU.
Nearly three years since Apple introduced these conditions in the EU and almost a year since Japan, not one developer has been able to launch a non-WebKit browser on iOS. The reason is simple: Apple’s conditions remain unworkable. The real test of any rule is whether it delivers the change it was meant to achieve in practice. Compliance on paper alone falls short of that test.
Since the DMA came into effect we’ve been working behind the scenes to test and improve Apple’s conditions so that we can bring Gecko to iOS users. Those efforts have so far largely failed.
One of the conditions requires us not to disclose non-public information related to the project, but here’s an example of what we are up against: to use Gecko, we can’t simply update the Firefox app people already use and love. We’d have to build a whole separate app, a second Firefox. Then we’d have to ask every Firefox user to download it, move their bookmarks, passwords and history across, and manually switch over, step by confusing step.
That’s a lot to ask of people who already made their choice. A significant proportion of people would understandably give up, including many who picked Firefox from the EU’s choice screen.
The conditions Apple imposes, including a level of user friction Apple would never accept for its own apps, mean that the open door feels more like a trap door.
Firefox users on iPhone chose an independent browser. They should get everything that comes with it, including Gecko, the last major browser engine not developed by a big tech company. We’ll keep pushing for a real level playing field, not an opening that drops independent browsers through the floor.

Title:Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights
View PDF HTML (experimental)
Abstract:A language model is trained once and frozen. Making it better takes another training run, or a prompt, a harness or extra computation at inference that is gone once the task ends. We show a third way: to our knowledge for the first time, a language model rewrites its own weights by its own reasoning and becomes better, with no gradients and no training. We call this self-rewriting: the model is its own optimizer. In earlier work, a frontier model edited a much smaller one; here an open 27B model, given its weights, its graded answers and the ability to run itself, diagnoses its own failure and rewrites the weights behind it. It surpasses itself: its own edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on problems it never saw. It keeps getting better: it tests its edits on practice problems and submits only what works, each edit improving on the last, and over eight consecutive sessions its accuracy only rises. It repairs a damaged copy of itself, like a broken robot that fixes itself without being rebuilt: the copy solves almost nothing, yet with no retraining and without ever reading the original weights, the model brings it back to over 80% of the original accuracy on image tasks it never saw. And the ability emerges with scale: in one model family, the smaller models make no edit or break themselves, and only the largest improves and repairs itself. Unlike test-time scaling, self-rewriting writes its gain into the weights, at no cost per answer; unlike training, it runs only forward passes, as serving does. The wall between training and inference is a limit of our tools, not a property of intelligence. We believe self-rewriting is a necessary step toward recursive self-improvement: a model that, like a human, does not separate training from inference could keep upgrading itself for life, with no larger model, no harness or prompt engineering, and no pre- or post-training.

コードを書かなくなると、レビューの能力も落ちるIf you stop writing code, you also get worse at reviewing it

Whatever skill you’re learning, it won’t take long before someone with experience in that particular field tells you to get your hands dirty. It’s fine to read “Born to Run”, but you won’t be running an ultra marathon any time soon if you don’t start running. Whether it’s muscle memory, technique, physical fitness or problem solving you need to work on to reach your goals, you need to practice. This isn’t a controversial statement in any field at all, and has never really been.
Except in software engineering, somehow.
When it comes to software engineering, we somehow lost our way, thinking that our skills won’t stop improving if we stop practicing. Making such a claim in any other field would be sneered at at best. Imagine John Scofield1 saying he stopped practicing his guitar because “he was at a good enough level, musically, to maintain (and even improve!) his expertise” simply by 1) instructing others on how to play (either in vibe general terms, or with a strict spec musical notation), and then 2) listening to what they played and iterating until it sounded good enough for his next record. “My music is better”, he would claim, and moreover “actually playing the guitar was never the point anyway, there’s more to making music.”
Mind you, my point in this post is not to discuss the short term quality of code output (or recorded guitar playing, to continue the proverbial thread), but rather the skill of the craftsman practicing his craft vs the one who doesn’t practice at all.
To make another comparison that’s arguably closer to software engineering, or at least the “writing and reasoning about code” part of it, let’s look at authors in other fields. I have no doubt that reading great prose has the potential to nudge a writer towards better writing of his/her own; in fact it’s super important to have creative references to draw upon - no doubt at all. But, imagine an author who 1) stopped writing, and only pursued reading and giving feedback to other writers, and worse yet 2) mostly read AI generated prose!? To be perfectly honest, I would expect a quite steady (if not exponential) decline of this author’s writing skills.
“There’s more to software engineering than writing code” Link to heading
Sure. But if we can at least agree that the code that runs our software is a non-trivial part of it (though, granted, not all of it by far: The other parts of developing and maintaining software speak even louder against using generative AI as a significant tool service!), we should aspire to be proficient in understanding how code works, what good code looks like, what to watch out for and how software architecture plays a vital part in making code Easy To Change.
Simply “reviewing all the code Claude produced” won’t quite cut it. And who are you trying to fool, after all; at this point you’re not really reading half of it, are you?
So I propose something that used to be a no-brainer: Keep practicing!
Which brings me to my next point:
All engineers(!) need to be good at solving problems Link to heading
if || while you can argue that the need for being hands-on on your software’s code varies, I propose you can’t argue that we’re still solving problems. And the moment you stop practicing solving problems is the moment you stop getting better at it, just like everything else. Simple as that. This is hardly new, btw: you could always tell who beat Doom using cheat codes and who finished the game the real way! If the end goal is to “beat the game”, then by all means cheat; if the end goal is getting good at it, then I’d advice you not to. Some lessons are only learned the hard way (trial and error), and very few are learned without effort at all. Repeated LLM usage for problem solving pounds the following premise into our brain’s wiring: If something is hard, tricky or requires effort, you shouldn’t even try. And right there goes our learning, craft and joy, right out the window.
In a world where people are using AI to summarise books instead of reading them and turning to AI for therapy, I don’t want to keep using it. It feels like such a slippery slope. As literacy and numeracy rates decline, I can’t help but question whether encouraging us to rely on AI so heavily is a good idea.
It makes me wonder: are we still thinking for ourselves at all?

TLDR; We built Blobasaur, a sharded, distributed, lightweight blob storage server on top of SQLite to solve our high-throughput blob read/write usecase. JSON blobs varying from a few KBs to MBs are now written and read thousands of times per second on commodity hardware while maintaining single-digit millisecond latency.
On Kite, our trading platform with ~20 million customers, users are able to customize their investment and trading screens heavily. They save multi-chart layouts, financial indicator templates, vector drawings, and various kinds of arbitrary UI preferences. In addition, we offer a Terminal mode that allows users to design their own workspaces by dragging, dropping, and customizing widgets. Under the hood, our backend services store these user preferences and metadata as opaque blobs (mostly JSON) ranging from a few kilobytes to several megabytes.
Many years ago, we stored such blobs in CouchDB, which turned out to be ill-suited for the kind of throughput we were starting to see. We then moved to SSDB (ssdb), a C++ disk-backed store that speaks the Redis protocol. This made it very easy to integrate as well. SSDB solved our immediate performance problems and ran reliably in production for several years.
Over time, two problems forced us to look for a replacement:
- SSDB became unmaintained, which was an obvious operational risk. It is written in C++, which is not a part of our stack or expertise, so forking and maintaining was also not viable.
- Scaling and size limits. SSDB is not a distributed system and can only run on a single node. This imposes significant future scaling challenges, including at one point forcing us to cap blob sizes at 1 MB because latency degrades on larger payloads.
As we added features like the Terminal mode and onboarded more internal services, we needed a robust solution. Our criteria were:
- A key-value blob store with a simple API.
- Designed for multi-megabyte blob read/write ops and high throughput. We sustain loads of thousands to tens of thousands of requests per second reading and writing arbitrary blobs. These are unpredictable and bursty thanks to the volatility in stock markets.
- Horizontally scalable, distributed.
- Disk-space efficiency / compression.
- Easily maintainable and highly economical.
So we investigated FOSS databases optimized for high-throughput blob storage and looked at the following:
- ScyllaDB: We already run ScyllaDB for high-throughput workloads, but in our tests, it turned out to be suboptimal for large blobs. Significantly lower throughput and higher resource usage. We specifically want multi-megabyte blobs. Tombstones from frequent overwrites and TTL expiries require careful tuning in ScyllaDB.
- PostgreSQL: We already have considerable experience with Postgres in our stack and experimented with storing blobs as BYTEA columns, but it turned out to be severely suboptimal again for the use case in terms of performance and disk-space efficiency.
- YugabyteDB: Has blob storage as one of the options, but is not primarily designed or optimized for it. Also, a large system with too many features.
- RocksDB: Had the right primitives, but again, not architected for high throughput multi-MB blob writes.
- Ceph: Severe operational complexity and resource requirements. We didn’t even get to trying it out.
- SeaweedFS: This was the closest candidate. However, there were a few blockers. Our use case is overwrite-heavy (a user changing their workspace) over the same key, and SeaweedFS keeps appending before compaction, which can cause disk usage to grow uncontrollably. Blobs have their own IDs, which then have to be mapped against user keys in a separate DB, adding another layer. It doesn’t support TTL per blob. Also, reasonably complex setup with many parts.
- S3 and MinIO: Object storage has high per-request latency for small blobs, high request costs under write-heavy workloads (writes are 50% of our traffic), and coarse lifecycle TTLs instead of exact key expiry. Not to mention, S3 would incur significant costs given thousands of ops per second and tens of millions of ops per day.
- Raw filesystem (one file per blob): Putting blobs directly on disk meant implementing atomic writes and write batching, managing large numbers of files and directories, good old atomic read/write disk performance and inode headaches, and maintaining a separate metadata index for TTLs. Not to mention, orchestrating this over AWS storage volumes.

先日パブリックプレビューが始まった次期サーバーOS「Windows Server 2028」には、削除・非推奨となる機能がある。米Microsoftが自社のドキュメントサイトで明らかにしている。
削除される機能
「Windows Server 2028」で削除される主な機能は、以下の通り。
- 「WINS」（Windows Internet Name Service）サーバーの用途：DNSベースの名前解決へ移行
- 「DirectAccess」：「Always On VPN」へ移行
- PPTP/L2TPプロトコルによるリモートアクセス：IKEv2またはSSTPへ移行
- 「SMB 1.0」：「SMB 2.0/3.0」へ移行
- 「VBScript」：管理スクリプトは「PowerShell」、Webのスクリプトは「JavaScript」へ移行
- 「WMIC」（Windows Management Instrumentation Command-line）ユーティリティ：WMI自体は削除されない。「PowerShell」やプログラムからのWMIクエリへ置き換え
- 「Windows Server Update Services」（WSUS）サーバーの用途
- PC/AT（PCAT）とレガシーBIOSによる起動：UEFIが必須に。「Hyper-V」でWindows Server 2028を動かすゲストVMも第2世代が必要。旧OSを動かす第1世代VMのサポートには影響しない
- 「MultiPoint Services」：「リモート デスクトップ サービス」へ移行
- 「Shielded VMs」：要件を満たす場合は「Trusted VMs」へ移行（ただし、プレビュー段階の「Trusted VMs」はフェイルオーバークラスターや「Hyper-V レプリカ」に未対応）
- 「AT Time Service」（ATSVC）と「at.exe」：「schtasks」を利用
- 「.NET Framework 3.5」のオプションコンポーネント：スタンドアロンインストーラーでは引き続き入手可能
影響が大きそうなのはWSUSだ。ただし、削除は「Windows Server 2028」に限られ、「Windows Server 2025」など旧版では各製品のサポート期間中、引き続き利用できる。Microsoftは、閉域網などでの更新管理についても今後情報を共有するとしている。

- 「BranchCache」
- 「Windows 展開サービス」（WDS）サーバーの用途：WDSが提供するPXE機能を含む。「Configuration Manager」でWDS経由のPXEを利用している場合は、WDS不要のPXEレスポンダーへ移行
- 「Windows PowerShell 5.1」：「PowerShell 7」への移行を計画するよう案内されている

ウェブブラウザで大量のタブを開いたり複数のアプリを同時に動かしたりすると、PCのメモリ(RAM)が不足して動作が遅くなることがあります。メモリ不足を解消するにはRAMの増設が効果的ですが、CPUを使ってメモリ内のデータを圧縮すればRAMを増設せずに多くのデータを保持できるとのこと。ソフトウェアエンジニアのテリー・ファン・ネールフェン氏がLinuxにおけるメモリ圧縮の効果を実際に検証しています。
Do not buy RAM, compress! | Therry van Neerven
https://curiousbits.nl/blog/do-not-buy-ram-compress/
近年はAIの普及によって複数の作業を並行して進める機会が増えており、PCに求められるメモリ容量も増加しています。一方でRAMは高価であり、ノートPCなどでは購入後に増設できない場合もあります。
メモリが不足するとOSは使用頻度の低いデータをSSDなどのストレージに一時退避させますが、ストレージへの読み書きはRAMへのアクセスよりも時間がかかるため、アプリの切り替えや処理の実行が遅くなる原因となります。
「メモリ圧縮」はメモリ不足による性能低下を抑える方法のひとつ。RAM上のデータを小さくまとめることで、限られた容量のRAMにより多くのデータを格納します。データの圧縮や展開にはCPUの処理能力が必要となり、一部の処理ではわずかな遅延が発生しますが、ストレージへのアクセスを減らせるためPC全体の動作が改善する可能性があります。ファン・ネールフェン氏によると、データの種類によっては元の4分の1程度まで圧縮できることも珍しくないとのこと。
メモリ圧縮はWindowsやmacOSではすでに一般的な機能ですが、Linuxでは設定次第で活用の余地があるとのこと。Linuxはディストリビューションによってメモリ圧縮の採用状況が異なり、ファン・ネールフェン氏によるとFedoraやPop!_OSでは標準で利用できるものの、Ubuntuでは標準で有効になっていないそう。
ファン・ネールフェン氏はメモリ圧縮の効果を確かめるため、RAM内に圧縮されたデータを保存するLinuxの機能「zram」を使用。32GBのRAMを搭載したPCでFirefoxを起動し、ニュースサイトやSNS、動画配信サイトなど大量のウェブページを開きました。
大量のタブを開いた結果、実行中のアプリが要求するメモリ容量の合計は物理RAMの容量を超えていましたが、使用頻度の低いデータの一部がzramによって圧縮されることでデータがRAM内に保持されていたとのこと。
以下の画像はファン・ネールフェン氏が公開したzramの使用状況です。圧縮前は約5.7GBあったデータが約1.7GBのRAMに収まっていることが分かります。圧縮率は約3.35対1で、約4GB分のRAM使用量を削減できた計算です。
ファン・ネールフェン氏はメモリ圧縮によって手持ちのハードウェアをさらに有効活用できると指摘。過去にクラウド上でアプリを運用していた際にもCPUの負荷は低いままメモリ容量が不足していたことがあり、メモリ圧縮を利用していればサーバーの増強を避け、クラウドの費用を半減できた可能性があると述べています。
なお、Windowsでメモリ圧縮の状況を確認するにはタスクマネージャーの「パフォーマンス」にある「メモリ」を開けばOK。「使用中(圧縮)」の欄に圧縮されたメモリの量が表示されます。

おはようございます！こんにちは！おやすみなさい！NOTA で Corp IT（情シス）を担当している kajinari です。
この 1 年で、Corp IT の日々の運用作業のほとんどを Claude の Skill として定義しました。アカウントの発行、クラウドサービス権限の付与、クラウドサービスの設定変更、PC や iPhone の手配、Zoom Phone の払い出し、各種台帳の更新まで、AI が作業して依頼者への報告まで行います。
この記事では、AI に任せるようになった理由と任せている仕事、それを支える AI の育て方とチームへの広げ方を紹介します。
なぜ AI に任せるのか
NOTA は社員数が増え続けています。2025 年 6 月には 322 名でした。それが 2026 年 9 月には 564 名（常勤アルバイトを含む）になりました。
管理する SaaS や社員の数が増えると、運用の負荷は想像以上に増えます。アカウントの作成・削除、権限の付け替え、デバイスの手配といった作業は、人数 × SaaS の数だけ発生するからです。
IT Media 様主催の講演「クラウド時代のモダンな情シスの全体像」から抜粋します。
一方で、Corp ITの人数は同じペースでは増えません。
というかNOTAのCorp ITチームは わずか 3名しか いません（笑）！　
そこで、ルーティンのオペレーションはできるだけ AI が実行する形を目指しました。とはいえ、AI に丸投げしているわけではありません。人は要所で確認しますし、AI が取り消せない操作をするときは、必ず人が確認してから実行します。
これを実現できているのは、AI が操作しやすい環境を先に整えてきたからです。
例えば
- Okta を IdP（社内のログインとアカウントを一元管理する仕組み）にしている。
- そのため、SaaS のアカウント発行や権限の付与は、ほぼ Okta のグループに追加するだけで完結します。
- IdP連携していないものは、API経由でアカウント作成する
- クラウドサービスの設定変更系もAPI経由で操作できるように整えております。

AI をどう育てているか
Skill を作るのは、マネージャーとして振る舞うのに似ています。仕事の定義と要件を決め、仕事を分解し、それぞれの完了の条件を決めて伝えます。
1 つの Skill には 1 つの仕事を任せる
Skill は小さく作り、それぞれの責務と関連性を明確にします。そのうえで、束ね役の Skill から個別の Skill に仕事を渡します。部下の役割を決め、仕事を分けて任せるのと同じです。
人が止める場面を決めておく
AI に任せる範囲は、操作のリスクと影響範囲で決めています。社外への送信、取り消せない操作、方針によって結果が大きく変わる判断の 3 つだけは人が確認し、それ以外は止めずに実行させます。確認を挟みすぎるのも、人の時間を使うコストだからです。
デリゲーションポーカーで、自律的に行動できるようにあらかじめ整理する作業にも似ています。

成功の返事をどこまで信じるか決めておく
API が成功を返しても、実際には反映されていないことがあります。既定では成功の返事を信じ、反映されなかったことを実際に確かめた API だけ、直後に読み直させます。
定義と実際の動きの食い違いを毎回確かめる
Skill の定義と実際の設定が食い違っていたら、実際の設定に合わせて作業させ、食い違いは作業の最後にすべて報告させます。どちらを正しいとするかは、報告を見て人が判断し、Skill の定義を直します。実行して、確かめて、すぐ直す。このフィードバックループを小さく回すのが、いちばん大事だと感じています。
出来事ではなくルールを書く
失敗したときは、何が起きたかを記録するのではなく、同じことが起きないルールを書きます。ルール同士が矛盾したら、追記で補わず、矛盾する側を消します。AI は矛盾を見つけると、確認する方を選んで止まってしまうからです。 人が常に実現したい価値は何かに焦点を当てると、自然とシンプルで無駄のない運用を定義し続けられます。この部分はAIに任せず、人が仕組みを作ります。

Skill を作り始めたころは、1 つの Skill にいろいろな仕事を載せてしまい、うまく動かないことがありました。その経験から、いまは最初に「Skill を作るための Skill」を育てるようにしています。
この章で書いてきたことは、まさにその中身です。何本も Skill を作るなかで学んだことを、Skill の作り方の決まりに書き戻します。このループを回すことで、質のよい Skill を量産できるようになりました。AI の仕事の質は、結局はハーネス（AI に渡すルールや手順、道具の整え方）次第だと思っています。

Today we are releasing Inkling 2 Mini, a fast, powerful model that’s small enough to run on your laptop.
Inkling 2 Mini is a looped transformer, running the middle layers of the model three times for every token. This allows the model to apply more reasoning and reach the right answer with fewer tokens, especially on tasks such as agentic coding that depend on reasoning quality. Inkling 2 Mini leads every open-weights model with less than 100B total parameters on benchmarks such as DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%). It runs faster than similarly performing models as well.
Inkling 2 Mini is a Mixture-of-Experts model with 26.4B total parameters, 3.4B active, and 5.4B weight activations per token to account for looping. Looping allows the model to reason longer without writing its reasoning down, which matters for monitoring: our ability to oversee and understand what the model does by reading its chain of thought (CoT).
We measured the impact of looping on both performance and monitorability with Inkling 2 Mini and a research-specific variable-loop variant we are releasing alongside it. The variable-loop model’s no-CoT time horizon improved from 16 seconds at one pass to 37 seconds with three, and its performance on a benchmark of serial reasoning kept improving out to ten loops—twice as many as it was trained with. The model’s chain of thought grew shorter with more loops but stayed legible throughout. The model didn’t trend higher on tests of agentic sabotage as loops were added.
We’re excited to offer some of the first models with looped architecture available to researchers. The variable-loop model was used for our monitorability experiments as it allows for cleaner comparisons between different looping depths.
Most of Inkling 2 Mini’s weights were trained and are deployed natively in NVFP4, with the last four layers in MXFP8. The total memory footprint of Inkling 2 Mini is ~20GB, allowing it to run on local devices such as consumer laptops and desktop AI computers. Our researchers took Inkling 2 Mini on a flight, where it ran offline on a MacBook Pro and built a playable flight simulator in a few minutes.
Inkling 2 Mini is released with open weights and is available on Tinker today for inference and customization.

Title:Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights
View PDF HTML (experimental)
Abstract:A language model is trained once and frozen. Making it better takes another training run, or a prompt, a harness or extra computation at inference that is gone once the task ends. We show a third way: to our knowledge for the first time, a language model rewrites its own weights by its own reasoning and becomes better, with no gradients and no training. We call this self-rewriting: the model is its own optimizer. In earlier work, a frontier model edited a much smaller one; here an open 27B model, given its weights, its graded answers and the ability to run itself, diagnoses its own failure and rewrites the weights behind it. It surpasses itself: its own edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on problems it never saw. It keeps getting better: it tests its edits on practice problems and submits only what works, each edit improving on the last, and over eight consecutive sessions its accuracy only rises. It repairs a damaged copy of itself, like a broken robot that fixes itself without being rebuilt: the copy solves almost nothing, yet with no retraining and without ever reading the original weights, the model brings it back to over 80% of the original accuracy on image tasks it never saw. And the ability emerges with scale: in one model family, the smaller models make no edit or break themselves, and only the largest improves and repairs itself. Unlike test-time scaling, self-rewriting writes its gain into the weights, at no cost per answer; unlike training, it runs only forward passes, as serving does. The wall between training and inference is a limit of our tools, not a property of intelligence. We believe self-rewriting is a necessary step toward recursive self-improvement: a model that, like a human, does not separate training from inference could keep upgrading itself for life, with no larger model, no harness or prompt engineering, and no pre- or post-training.

On Kite, our trading platform with ~20 million customers, users are able to customize their investment and trading screens heavily. SSDB solved our immediate performance problems and ran reliably in production for several years.

- SSDB became unmaintained, which was an obvious operational risk. It is written in C++, which is not a part of our stack or expertise, so forking and maintaining was also not viable.
- Scaling and size limits. SSDB is not a distributed system and can only run on a single node. This imposes significant future scaling challenges, including at one point forcing us to cap blob sizes at 1 MB because latency degrades on larger payloads.

- A key-value blob store with a simple API.
- Designed for multi-megabyte blob read/write ops and high throughput. We sustain loads of thousands to tens of thousands of requests per second reading and writing arbitrary blobs. These are unpredictable and bursty thanks to the volatility in stock markets.
- Horizontally scalable, distributed.
- Disk-space efficiency / compression.
- Easily maintainable and highly economical.

- YugabyteDB: Has blob storage as one of the options, but is not primarily designed or optimized for it. Also, a large system with too many features.
- RocksDB: Had the right primitives, but again, not architected for high throughput multi-MB blob writes.
- Ceph: Severe operational complexity and resource requirements. We didn’t even get to trying it out.

メモリが不足するとOSは使用頻度の低いデータをSSDなどのストレージに一時退避させますが、ストレージへの読み書きはRAMへのアクセスよりも時間がかかるため、アプリの切り替えや処理の実行が遅くなる原因となります。
「メモリ圧縮」はメモリ不足による性能低下を抑える方法のひとつ。RAM上のデータを小さくまとめることで、限られた容量のRAMにより多くのデータを格納します。データの圧縮や展開にはCPUの処理能力が必要となり、一部の処理ではわずかな遅延が発生しますが、ストレージへのアクセスを減らせるためPC全体の動作が改善する可能性があります。ファン・ネールフェン氏によると、データの種類によっては元の4分の1程度まで圧縮できることも珍しくないとのこと。
メモリ圧縮はWindowsやmacOSではすでに一般的な機能ですが、Linuxでは設定次第で活用の余地があるとのこと。Linuxはディストリビューションによってメモリ圧縮の採用状況が異なり、ファン・ネールフェン氏によるとFedoraやPop!_OSでは標準で利用できるものの、Ubuntuでは標準で有効になっていないそう。
ファン・ネールフェン氏はメモリ圧縮の効果を確かめるため、RAM内に圧縮されたデータを保存するLinuxの機能「zram」を使用。32GBのRAMを搭載したPCでFirefoxを起動し、ニュースサイトやSNS、動画配信サイトなど大量のウェブページを開きました。

Whatever skill you’re learning, it won’t take long before someone with experience in that particular field tells you to get your hands dirty. It’s fine to read “Born to Run”, but you won’t be running an ultra marathon any time soon if you don’t start running. Whether it’s muscle memory, technique, physical fitness or problem solving you need to work on to reach your goals, you need to practice. This isn’t a controversial statement in any field at all, and has never really been.
Except in software engineering, somehow.
When it comes to software engineering, we somehow lost our way, thinking that our skills won’t stop improving if we stop practicing. Making such a claim in any other field would be sneered at at best. Imagine John Scofield1 saying he stopped practicing his guitar because “he was at a good enough level, musically, to maintain (and even improve!) his expertise” simply by 1) instructing others on how to play (either in vibe general terms, or with a strict spec musical notation), and then 2) listening to what they played and iterating until it sounded good enough for his next record. “My music is better”, he would claim, and moreover “actually playing the guitar was never the point anyway, there’s more to making music.”
Mind you, my point in this post is not to discuss the short term quality of code output (or recorded guitar playing, to continue the proverbial thread), but rather the skill of the craftsman practicing his craft vs the one who doesn’t practice at all.
To make another comparison that’s arguably closer to software engineering, or at least the “writing and reasoning about code” part of it, let’s look at authors in other fields. I have no doubt that reading great prose has the potential to nudge a writer towards better writing of his/her own; in fact it’s super important to have creative references to draw upon - no doubt at all. But, imagine an author who 1) stopped writing, and only pursued reading and giving feedback to other writers, and worse yet 2) mostly read AI generated prose!? To be perfectly honest, I would expect a quite steady (if not exponential) decline of this author’s writing skills.
“There’s more to software engineering than writing code” Link to heading
Sure. But if we can at least agree that the code that runs our software is a non-trivial part of it (though, granted, not all of it by far: The other parts of developing and maintaining software speak even louder against using generative AI as a significant tool service!), we should aspire to be proficient in understanding how code works, what good code looks like, what to watch out for and how software architecture plays a vital part in making code Easy To Change.
Simply “reviewing all the code Claude produced” won’t quite cut it. And who are you trying to fool, after all; at this point you’re not really reading half of it, are you?
So I propose something that used to be a no-brainer: Keep practicing!
Which brings me to my next point:
All engineers(!) need to be good at solving problems Link to heading
if || while you can argue that the need for being hands-on on your software’s code varies, I propose you can’t argue that we’re still solving problems. And the moment you stop practicing solving problems is the moment you stop getting better at it, just like everything else. Simple as that. This is hardly new, btw: you could always tell who beat Doom using cheat codes and who finished the game the real way! If the end goal is to “beat the game”, then by all means cheat; if the end goal is getting good at it, then I’d advice you not to. Some lessons are only learned the hard way (trial and error), and very few are learned without effort at all. Repeated LLM usage for problem solving pounds the following premise into our brain’s wiring: If something is hard, tricky or requires effort, you shouldn’t even try. And right there goes our learning, craft and joy, right out the window.
In a world where people are using AI to summarise books instead of reading them and turning to AI for therapy, I don’t want to keep using it. It feels like such a slippery slope. As literacy and numeracy rates decline, I can’t help but question whether encouraging us to rely on AI so heavily is a good idea.
It makes me wonder: are we still thinking for ourselves at all?

Why there are still no non-WebKit browsers on iOS
Firefox on iPhone is growing fast across the world. This is especially true in the EU since 2024, when the EU Digital Markets Act (DMA) started requiring Apple to let people choose their default browser; millions have picked Firefox, and daily use on iOS has doubled. People want to choose the browser on their phone.
But there remains an important limitation to what is available on iPhones: Apple forces all browsers on iOS to be built on Apple’s own browser engine.
This has meant that companies like Mozilla cannot easily bring the full range of Firefox features and functionality to iPhone users because many of these are built within our browser engine, Gecko. iPhones and iPads are unique in having this restriction. On all other major operating systems, including Apple’s macOS, people can access the Gecko-powered version of Firefox.
As a result of the DMA, Apple began allowing alternative browser engines on iOS in the EU. It published a set of conditions that companies like Mozilla must comply with to bring their own browser engine. At the time we said Apple’s rules made it “as painful as possible” for alternatives like Gecko. In the meantime, Japan also introduced new rules requiring alternative browser engines on iOS. Apple applied very similar conditions in Japan to those in the EU.
Nearly three years since Apple introduced these conditions in the EU and almost a year since Japan, not one developer has been able to launch a non-WebKit browser on iOS. The reason is simple: Apple’s conditions remain unworkable. The real test of any rule is whether it delivers the change it was meant to achieve in practice. Compliance on paper alone falls short of that test.
Since the DMA came into effect we’ve been working behind the scenes to test and improve Apple’s conditions so that we can bring Gecko to iOS users. Those efforts have so far largely failed.
One of the conditions requires us not to disclose non-public information related to the project, but here’s an example of what we are up against: to use Gecko, we can’t simply update the Firefox app people already use and love. We’d have to build a whole separate app, a second Firefox. Then we’d have to ask every Firefox user to download it, move their bookmarks, passwords and history across, and manually switch over, step by confusing step.
That’s a lot to ask of people who already made their choice. A significant proportion of people would understandably give up, including many who picked Firefox from the EU’s choice screen.
The conditions Apple imposes, including a level of user friction Apple would never accept for its own apps, mean that the open door feels more like a trap door.
Firefox users on iPhone chose an independent browser. They should get everything that comes with it, including Gecko, the last major browser engine not developed by a big tech company. We’ll keep pushing for a real level playing field, not an opening that drops independent browsers through the floor.

政府、サイバー攻撃「緊急対策パッケージ」に強制遮断「ブロッキング」の検討盛り込む方針…法的課題の検討急ぐ

政府が近くまとめるサイバーセキュリティー緊急対策パッケージに、二次被害の防止対策として、ウェブサイトへの接続を強制的に遮断する「ブロッキング」の検討が盛り込まれることが分かった。「アクセス抑止策」として検討する。ＧＭＯインターネットの不正アクセス問題を巡って攻撃者が個人情報をサイトで公開する事例が発生している。漏えいした個人情報の悪用を防ぐため、ブロッキングをはじめとする対策の法的課題などを早急に検討する方針を示す。
ブロッキングは、特定のサイトへの接続を強制的に遮断し、サイトを閲覧できなくする措置だ。ＧＭＯインターネットの不正アクセス問題を巡り、攻撃者が１０月２７日、不正に取得した個人情報をサイトで公開。総務省は「二次被害が拡大する現実的な恐れが生じている」などとして、同日付で国内の通信事業者に対し、サイトへのブロッキングを要請した。
今回の総務省の要請は妥当だったとする意見がある一方で、拙速だったとの批判も出ている。ブロッキングを実施するには、通信事業者が全利用者のすべての通信先を確認する必要がある。通信内容や宛先などを第三者に知られない権利は「通信の秘密」として憲法などで保障されており、これまでも実施に当たって慎重に検討が進められてきた経緯があるためだ。
一方、サイバー攻撃による相次ぐ個人情報の漏えいで、今後も攻撃者が個人情報を公開するといった行為に出る恐れがある。政府はブロッキングなどの対策を機動的に講じることができるよう、法的課題の検討を急ぐことにした。
